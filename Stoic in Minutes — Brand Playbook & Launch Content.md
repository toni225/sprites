# Stoic in Minutes — Brand Playbook & Launch Content

*Ancient wisdom for modern problems.*

Oct 1, 2026 · @Toni Ross Inot

## Brand foundation

Stoic in Minutes helps people handle one real modern problem per video, using one Stoic idea they can apply today. Every post should leave the viewer with an action, not just a feeling.

**Positioning line (for bios):** One modern problem. One Stoic principle. One thing to do about it.

**What makes us different from quote pages:** we start with the problem, we cite our sources honestly, and we never confuse calm with passivity.

### Format: text-only videos

No voiceover. Viewers read short text slides over classical and modern visuals while instrumental music plays underneath. This works with the sound off (many people scroll muted), feels meditative, and is faster to produce than recorded narration.

The structure stays HOOK → MODERN PROBLEM → STOIC PRINCIPLE → PRACTICAL APPLICATION → TAKEAWAY, told across 9–12 slides. A 30–60 second video holds about 50–90 words in total, much less than a spoken script, so every line must earn its place.

**Pacing rule:** show each slide for 2 seconds, plus about 0.3 seconds per word. An 8-word slide stays up about 4.5 seconds; a quote card, 6–7 seconds. To test it, read each slide slowly in your head. If you finish just as it changes, the timing is right.

**First frame:** the hook text is on screen from frame one. No logo intro, no blank fade-in.

### Music

| Element | Guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Mood | Slow, low, and sparse: solo piano, cello or low strings, ambient drones, soft choir pads |
| Tempo | 60–80 BPM; no lyrics, no beat drops, nothing that builds to a climax |
| Signature sound | Pick one or two tracks as the brand's theme and use them on about a third of videos, so the sound becomes recognizable |
| Mix | Fade in over 1 second; fade out under the end card |
| Search terms | "dark ambient piano", "slow cinematic cello", "meditative strings", "ancient ambient" |

**Licensing matters when you cross-post.** Trending TikTok or Instagram sounds are licensed only inside that app, and business accounts on Instagram and Facebook are generally limited to each platform's commercial music library. If you export one file for all four platforms, use music licensed everywhere: the YouTube Audio Library (YouTube only), Pixabay Music (free, check each track's terms), or a paid library such as Epidemic Sound or Artlist that covers social platforms.

### Voice rules

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Open with a situation the viewer is in right now ("You've checked your phone six times.") | Open with "Marcus Aurelius once said..." |
| Short lines. Write like a calm friend who has read a lot. | Hype, shouting, "grindset" energy, "Alpha" framing |
| Acknowledge the feeling, then redirect the judgment | Imply emotions are weakness or should be suppressed |
| Give one concrete action per video | Stack five tips in 45 seconds |
| Draw a line where needed: calm is not the same as tolerating mistreatment | Tell people to "accept" abuse, harassment, or injustice |
| Label quotes as quote, paraphrase, or interpretation | Use unsourced quote graphics |

**Signature end card (every short):** "STOIC IN MINUTES" in gold Cinzel over "Ancient wisdom. Modern problems." in cream, on charcoal, for 2 seconds. Identical every time so it builds recognition.

**The ancient/modern cut:** the signature visual move is a hard cut between a classical image and a modern one. For example, a marble bust, then a phone lighting up on a nightstand. Use it in the first three seconds of most videos.

**Free image sources:** The Met Open Access, the Rijksmuseum, the Art Institute of Chicago, and Wikimedia Commons offer public-domain classical art. Check each image's license before use, and favor museum open-access collections over random search results.

### Typography

Since the text is the whole video, the fonts carry the brand. Use a Roman-inscription font for hooks, a screen-friendly serif for reading, and an elegant italic for quotes. All four are free on Google Fonts under the Open Font License, so commercial use is allowed.

| Role | Font | Weight | Size (1080×1920 canvas) | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hook and end card | **Cinzel** | SemiBold | 90–110 px | Modeled on Roman carved capitals; instantly reads as "ancient." All caps by design, so keep it to 6 words or fewer |
| Reading text (most slides) | **Lora** | Medium | 64–76 px | A serif designed for screens; stays clear and calm at phone size |
| Quote cards | **Cormorant Garamond** | SemiBold Italic | 72–84 px | Elegant and literary. Lighter weights get too thin on phones |
| Source lines and labels | **Inter** | Regular | 36–40 px, +5% letter spacing | Small and clean for "Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5" |

**Alternatives:** Playfair Display for hooks if Cinzel feels too formal; EB Garamond for reading text if you want a more bookish look; Montserrat in place of Inter.

**Where to get them:** Canva's font library includes these Google Fonts. For CapCut or other editors, check the built-in font list first, or download them free from fonts.google.com.

### Text layout rules

- **One idea per slide, 12 words maximum.** Break long sentences across two slides rather than shrinking the type.
- **Gold for 1–2 key words only** per slide; everything else in cream. (In the scripts below, **bold** = gold.)
- **Readable over images:** put a 50–60% black overlay on any photo behind text, or place text on solid charcoal.
- **Stay in the safe zone:** keep text in the middle of the frame, clear of the bottom 20% and right 10%, where captions and buttons cover the video.
- **Gentle motion only:** a 0.3–0.5 second fade or slow rise. No typewriter effects, bounces, or shakes.

## Quote integrity system

Accurate sourcing is your competitive advantage: most Stoic pages recycle fake quotes, so being the page that gets it right builds trust quickly.

**Important:** the passages below were written from memory, not checked against a database. Verify every quote against the original translation before publishing. All the translations named are public domain and free online (Project Gutenberg, Wikisource).

### Three labels, used on screen and in captions

| Label | When to use | Example on screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quote** | Exact words from a named translation | "..." — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5 (trans. Long) |
| **Paraphrase** | Faithful restatement of a real passage | Epictetus, paraphrased (*Enchiridion* 25) |
| **Our take** | A modern application of the idea | No attribution; it's our voice |

### Recommended source translations

| Author | Work | Public-domain translation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marcus Aurelius | *Meditations* | George Long (1862) |
| Epictetus | *Enchiridion* (Handbook), *Discourses* | George Long, or Elizabeth Carter |
| Seneca | *Moral Letters to Lucilius* | Richard Mott Gummere (Loeb, 1917–25) |
| Seneca | *On Anger*, *On the Shortness of Life* | John W. Basore (Loeb, 1928–35) |

### Never use (common misattributions)

These circulate widely but don't appear in the Stoic texts. They make strong material for the Real or Fake? series.

- "Everything we hear is an opinion, not a fact. Everything we see is a perspective, not the truth." Attributed to Marcus Aurelius; not found in the *Meditations*.
- "A gem cannot be polished without friction, nor a man perfected without trials." Often credited to Seneca; it does not appear in his works.
- "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity." Often credited to Seneca; no source in his writings.

**Fact-check routine:** for any quote, search a distinctive phrase inside the Gutenberg text of the work. No match means it's a paraphrase at best. Label it accordingly or don't use it.

## Launch strategy: first 30 days

Post one short video a day for 30 days, filmed in batches of five, then launch long-form YouTube once you know which topics perform. Consistency matters more than polish in month one.

### Before you post anything

- [ ] Claim the handle "stoicinminutes" (or the closest available) on all four platforms on the same day
- [ ] Set up the bio: "Ancient wisdom for modern problems. One Stoic idea you can use today."
- [ ] Make the logo, a profile image, and one text-slide template in Canva or CapCut with the fonts, colors, and end card locked in using the visual system
- [ ] Build a library of 10–15 licensed instrumental tracks and choose one signature track
- [ ] Build a folder of 50+ licensed classical images and 50+ modern B-roll clips before day one

### The four weeks

1. **Week 1, Foundation.** Post scripts 1–7 below. These cover the core idea (control) plus the most searched emotional problems. Pin the "control your response" video to your profile.
2. **Week 2, Introduce series.** Post scripts 8–10, plus the first Real or Fake? and the first Memento Mori Monday. Reply to every comment, because early replies shape the algorithm and the community.
3. **Week 3, Double down.** Check which three videos have the best average watch time and the most saves. Make two follow-up shorts on each of those topics from new angles.
4. **Week 4, Go long.** Publish the first long-form YouTube video (below) on the best-performing theme. Cut three shorts from it that link back to the full video.

### Adapting one video across platforms

| Platform | Length | Adjustments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TikTok | 30–45 s | Hook in the first 1.5 s; native text overlays; reply to comments with video replies |
| Instagram Reels | 30–60 s | Strong cover frame in brand colors; turn the takeaway into a carousel post the same week |
| YouTube Shorts | 30–60 s | Title it like a search ("How to stop caring what people think"); link to the related long video |
| Facebook Reels | 45–60 s | Slightly slower pacing works; longer captions with the full takeaway perform well |

### What to measure

Ignore follower count for the first month. Track average watch percentage (aim for 60%+ on shorts), saves, and shares; those show whether the content is useful. Comments asking "what about when..." are your best source of new topics.

**\[0:00\] The conversation you keep replaying**

You said something in a meeting last week. Maybe a joke that didn't land. Maybe you stumbled over a sentence. Nobody reacted much. But you've replayed it, what, thirty times? Forty?

And every replay asks the same question: what do they think of me now?

If that sounds familiar, you're in good company. One of the most powerful men in history had the same problem. And he left notes on how he dealt with it.

*Visuals: person lying awake → phone screen lighting up → cut to marble bust. Title card.*

**\[0:55\] What an emperor noticed about all of us**

Marcus Aurelius ruled the Roman Empire for nearly two decades. He kept a private journal we now call the *Meditations*. It was never meant to be published. It's him, talking to himself.

In Book 12, he writes something that sounds like it was written this morning. He says he's often wondered how every person loves himself more than anyone else, "but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others."

Think about how strange that is. You know your intentions. You know how hard you tried. You know the context. Other people get a five-second glimpse. And yet their glimpse feels more real than your full picture.

An emperor noticed this in himself. Which tells you something: status doesn't fix it. More followers won't fix it. Something else has to.

*Visuals: Roman Forum ruins → slow pan across a manuscript page → on-screen text of the quote with the source: Meditations 12.4, trans. George Long.*

**\[2:30\] The two piles**

The Stoics had a tool for this, and it's the foundation of everything else they taught. Epictetus, a former slave who became one of the most respected teachers in Rome, opens his Handbook with it: "Of things some are in our power, and others are not."

Picture two piles.

In the first pile: your choices, your effort, your honesty, how you treat people, what you do next.

In the second pile: the weather, the economy, your past, and, this is the important one, what other people think.

You can influence opinions. You can explain yourself, apologize, do good work. But the final verdict happens inside someone else's mind, and you don't live there.

Here's the Stoic insight: most of our anxiety comes from treating pile-two things as if they were pile-one things. We try to control what we can only influence. And we neglect what we actually own.

*Visuals: animated two-column graphic in cream and gold; items drop into each pile one by one.*

**\[4:20\] Why opinions feel like threats**

It's worth being honest about why this is so hard. For most of human history, being rejected by your group was genuinely dangerous. Caring about belonging kept people alive. So the feeling isn't stupid. It's old.

The Stoics didn't ask you to stop feeling things. That's a common misunderstanding. They asked you to examine the judgments behind your feelings. Epictetus said people are disturbed "not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things."

So when someone frowns at your idea, the event is a frown. The judgment is "they think I'm an idiot, and that matters enormously." That second part is where the suffering comes from. And that second part is in your pile.

*Visuals: close-up of a frown → freeze frame → text overlay splits it into "Event" and "Judgment."*

**\[5:55\] Four Stoic practices**

So what do you actually do? Here are four practices drawn from Stoic texts, each turned into something you can use this week.

**One: the three-question audit.** When you catch yourself replaying, ask: Was I honest? Was I kind? Did I do my best with what I knew? If the answer is yes, the case is closed. If it's no, fix it directly with the person, once. Either way, stop the replay. This is our modern take on the two-piles idea.

**Two: be willing to look foolish.** Epictetus wrote: "If you would improve, be content to be thought foolish and stupid with respect to externals." Anyone learning anything looks clumsy at first. If you need to look impressive at every moment, you'll never try anything new. Pick one thing this week you'll do badly in public, on purpose.

**Three: the invisible test.** Before you post, speak, or decide, ask: would I still do this if I could never see the reaction? This separates what you value from what you're performing.

**Four: remember the scale.** Marcus repeatedly reminded himself how brief fame is, how the people praising us will soon be forgotten, and those who remember them too. Not to be bleak. To put things in proportion. The person judging you will think about that moment far less than you will.

*Visuals: each practice gets a gold numeral card; modern B-roll for each (notebook, a beginner at a climbing wall, a phone being placed face-down, a time-lapse of a crowd dispersing).*

**\[8:45\] Where this goes wrong**

One important caution. "Don't care what people think" can turn into "don't care about people." That's not Stoicism. The Stoics valued justice and our duties to others very highly.

Feedback from someone who knows your work, or a friend telling you that you hurt them: that deserves your attention. The goal isn't to stop listening. It's to stop handing over your self-worth.

*Visuals: two people talking honestly over coffee → statue of two figures side by side.*

**\[9:35\] The one question to keep**

So if you remember one thing, make it this question: is this mine to control?

If it is, act on it. If it isn't, let it go back to its owner.

Your reputation lives in other people's heads. Your character lives in yours. Spend your energy on the one you actually own.
